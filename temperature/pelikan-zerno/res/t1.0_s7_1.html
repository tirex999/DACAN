01 — The specimen

Built for
diving.
Modified
for pedalling.

Nothing about a pelican is aerodynamic. The bill is a two-metre fishing rod. The pouch is an eleven-litre net. The chest is a air-filled balloon the bird uses as a shock absorber when it hits the ocean at 40 km/h — which is, mechanically, the same job a saddle does.

Hover the plate to inspect the parts that make cycling possible. Nothing was trained. The bird walked onto the bike, sat down on the saddle, and has not been off it since 1998.

02 — Flight plan (grounded)

He flies twice a year.
The rest is commuting.

The route runs eleven kilometres of paved path between the pier and the estuary: flat, exposed, and directly into whatever the afternoon decides to do about the wind. He takes it in a low tuck, wings trussed along the frame, pouch clipped to the top tube so the fish stays level.

Panel A

The bird

Air sacs in the humeri, a spine built to survive a 40 km/h collision with the Pacific, and a resting heart rate of 60. On a bicycle he is simply a very large, very stable aerodynamic problem who has decided to solve it with his legs instead of his chest.

Panel B

The machine

Steel, 1998, single-speed, drop bars wrapped in eel skin. No brakes — the pelican's argument, delivered at every committee meeting for 27 years, is that a bird with a 2.3 m wingspan does not need a brake.

Panel C

The route

Eleven kilometres, eleven gulls, one headwind that arrives like a rumour and stays like a relative.

  1. Mount. Left foot on the pedal, right foot on the ground, wings folded with the ceremony of a man putting on a coat.
  2. Roll. Three hops to build speed. Pelicans need 8 km/h to become graceful, on land or in the air.
  3. Tuck. Neck retracted into the shoulders, bill along the top tube. Drag drops by roughly a third; the pouch, full, drops by none.
  4. Descend the estuary straight. Ninety seconds of open pavement, wind on the beam, cadence 96, nothing in the world behind him.
