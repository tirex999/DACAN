Anatomy of the descent

Five structures of interest, drawn to scale from a photograph taken at 1/2000 s. Hover a note to locate it.

  1. 01

    Crest tuft

    Nuptial plume, July only. Doubts remain as to whether it is ornamental or a wind gauge. It leans into weather.

  2. 02

    The great eye

    Field of view 320°. Blind spot: precisely one metre directly behind the saddle, which is where the traffic is.

  3. 03

    Cycle cap

    Not native to the species. Acquired at the market in Aigues-Mortes for two euros and a mackerel.

  4. 04

    Gular pouch

    Capacity 11.4 litres. On the day of observation: two mackerel, one prawn, and a tram receipt.

  5. 05

    Mandible nail

    The hooked tip. Used for signalling, fish, and the brake lever — which is not fitted.

“A pelican on a bicycle is not a joke. It is a commute.”

N–01

On the westerly

Subject faced a force-four wind off the sea and nonetheless gained three kilometres an hour on the descent to the pier. Pelicans do not descend; they arrive. This one, however, had clearly been practising.

The scarf streamed at eleven degrees above the horizontal, indicating a headwind of some 24 km/h and a bird quite unbothered by it.

N–02

On the pedalling

Leg short; crank long. The resulting cadence is best described as enthusiastic. Note that the ankle leads the stroke — a trait shared with herons, cranes, and every good climber.

Peak power at 105° of crank angle. The webbed foot does not slip from the pedal; the webbing, it appears, was designed for this.

N–03

On the bell

Rung forty-one times in six minutes. Traffic yielded without complaint. Two gulls followed the subject all the way home and were fed.

N–04

On the basket

Wicker, front-mounted, carrying one fish tail. The fish was not eaten during transit. It appeared to be a delivery.
