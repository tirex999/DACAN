§ i · Field record

Approached the promenade at speed, bell already ringing.

10:06. Wind off the water, 11 kt. The bird rolled out of the pier shadow on a steel‑frame cruiser, pouch full, tail feathers square to the wind, and held the seaward lane for two furlongs without a wobble. Pedalling is webbed and unhurried; the seat post works; the head is steady as a surveyor's. When the gulls crowded the breakwater he rang the bell and they dispersed. Stopped at the kiosk, bought a mackerel, rode home the long way.

Behavioural note. A pelican does not steer with the feet. It steers with the jaw, the wind, and a loud opinion about the bell.

§ ii · Anatomy of the rider

Everything on the bird is load‑bearing.

§ iii · The pedalling mechanism

A circle in the foot, an ellipse in the knee.

  1. Feet are clamped to the crank

    The pedal describes a 340 mm circle at a fixed 170 mm radius. The bird does not pull the foot back; the circle does all the negotiating.

  2. Hips stay on the saddle

    The hip is a fixed point, 132 mm above and 28 mm behind the bottom bracket. From there, the foot is not chosen — it is given.

  3. Knees solve themselves

    Thigh and shin have fixed lengths. Where two circles of those radii intersect — one centred on the hip, one on the pedal — the knee must live. Two intersections; birds choose the forward one.

  4. Seat posts forgive

    Two‑thirds of the vertical load is a spring in the seat post. This is why the bird's eye stays level while the frame jolts over shell‑crushed tarmac.

Pouch load · a note on sag
A full pouch is a suspended mass: it swings on the cadence clock, adding a few pixels of droop at the bottom of each pedal stroke. Drag the jiggle control under the plate to load it; at rest it lies flat against the lower mandible, which is, functionally, a chin curtain at speed.
Why the far leg is drawn first
In true side elevation the drive crank points toward the viewer, so its pedal is behind the frame and appears short and dull‑coloured. Engravers have always lied slightly and pushed the far leg forward; this plate is honest, and you can watch the two pedals trade places at the top and bottom of each revolution.

§ v · Cyclings notes for the webbed foot

Every pelican rides badly for the first fortnight. The trouble is the pouch: it wants to lead. It swings out in front of the head like a water‑glass carried by a nervous waiter, and the bird, alarmed, pedals harder — which makes it swing more. The cure is cadence. At ninety revolutions a minute the pouch settles into a slow, forgiving ellipse, the front wheel stops hunting, and the whole machine agrees with the tide.

Brake before the gulls, not at them. Ring on the brow of a dune; a bell has right of way by long coastal custom, and a bicycle with a bell and a pelican is an irresistible combination, which is to say the gulls will come, and the bell will get louder.

Rules of the promenade

  • Both feet on the pedals when passing the post office.
  • No pouch, no ride. Empty pouches flap; flapping pouches steer badly.
  • Fish tail visible, one hand's width above the bar — a courtesy signal.
  • Wing on the bar at all times; the brake lever has no thumb.
  • Give way to tide, gulls, children, and anyone with a net.
  • Ring twice at the kiosk. The mackerel is cash; the bell is character.
